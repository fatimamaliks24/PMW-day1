# Rohtas Fort: A Living Fortress on the Potohar Plateau

## The piece

Sher Shah Suri built Rohtas Fort in a hurry, and it shows in the best possible way. After beating the Mughal emperor Humayun in 1541, Sher Shah needed to lock down the route between Peshawar and Lahore and keep the rebellious Gakhar tribes of the Potohar region in check. So he raised a garrison fort on a ridge above the Kahan River — 4 kilometres of walls, 68 bastion towers, and twelve monumental gates, built fast, built to hold 30,000 men, and never once taken by force.

What makes Rohtas worth pointing a camera at from every angle is that it refuses to sit still stylistically. Sohail Gate, the ceremonial entrance, carries sunflower motifs and calligraphy panels that would look at home on a Mughal tomb. Shishi Gate is named for the glazed blue tiles on its outer arch — the earliest known use of a technique later perfected in Lahore. Khwas Khani Gate is almost a mirror image inside and out, a rare symmetry among the fort's entrances. And Langar Khani Gate was built as a trap: an L-shaped opening positioned in the direct line of fire from the bastions, so an intruder who broke through the outer arch would have to turn blind into a kill zone.

The fort's second layer of interest is that it's still inhabited. Roughly a third of the enclosed area is occupied by Rohtas village, lived in continuously since the 1550s — a government high school, a post office, seventeen mosques, and around 4,000 residents inside walls built for a 16th-century garrison. UNESCO listed the fort in 1997 as "an exceptional example of the Muslim military architecture of Central and South Asia," but it's also one of the few fortresses in the world where the World Heritage Site and the neighborhood are the same physical footprint. That tension — monument versus home, conservation plan versus 450 occupied houses — is itself part of the story a multi-angle documentation project should capture: wide establishing drone shots of the walls and gates, and close ground-level footage of the village life happening inside them.

Structurally, the fort rewards footage from height and from the ground equally. From the air, the irregular triangular footprint and the way the ramparts follow the hill's contours only make sense as a whole shape. At ground level, the machicolations, the carved Persian inscriptions dating construction to 1541 AH 948, and the stepwells (baolis) cut into limestone are details that a wide shot flattens into texture. A curation strategy for this subject should pair aerial establishing shots (walls, gate placement, the fort's relationship to the Kahan River gorge) with close interior passes (Sohail Gate's oriel windows, Shishi Gate's tilework, the Rani Mahal's flower-carved dome).

## Why the material matters

Rohtas is one of the best-preserved examples of pre-Mughal military architecture on the subcontinent, and its construction predates the aesthetic conventions — gardens, symmetry, ornamental excess — that later define Mughal forts like Lahore Fort or the Red Fort. Documenting it multi-angle is useful precisely because photographs alone tend to flatten it into "another old fort" shot; footage that moves through the gates, up the ramparts, and down into the baolis conveys the scale (18-metre walls, 13-metre thickness in places) and the functional logic (line-of-sight traps, molten-lead drains) that a single frame can't.

It's also a live conservation case. The Rohtas Fort Conservation Programme (Archaeology and Museums Department + Himalayan Wildlife Foundation, since 2000) is actively working against water damage — Talaqi Gate's inner face partially collapsed in 2005, and Gatali Gate's bastion has eroded from rain infiltration. Multi-angle footage taken now has documentary value beyond the module: it's a dated record of a monument that is still losing material to weather and encroachment.

### Curation notes
- Strongest material for the piece: the Wikimedia Commons gate subcategories (openly licensed, high resolution, already organized by structure) paired with the 4K aerial YouTube video for scale context.
- Weakest/skip: general "Rohtas" stock footage searches on Adobe Stock returned a mix of the Pakistani fort and the unrelated Rohtasgarh Fort in Bihar, India — confirm location before using any stock-footage result.
- Gap: no drone footage found that captures the Talaqi Gate collapse or Gatali Gate erosion damage specifically — if the module wants a conservation angle, that's a shooting gap rather than a sourcing gap.
