# Rohtas Fort: The Unconquered Fortress of the Sur Empire

## Introduction

Rising above a gorge near the Kahan River in Punjab, Pakistan, Rohtas Fort (Qila Rohtas) is one of the largest and best-preserved military fortifications in South Asia. Built in the mid-16th century, it has never fallen to a direct assault — a rare distinction for a fort of its age and scale. Today it stands as a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a case study in both the strengths and struggles of heritage conservation in Pakistan.

## Origins and Construction

The fort was commissioned in 1541 by Sher Shah Suri, the Pashtun founder of the Sur Empire, shortly after he defeated the Mughal emperor Humayun at the Battle of Kannauj and needed to block Humayun's route back into India after his exile to Persia. Sher Shah entrusted the project to his revenue minister, Raja Todar Mal, who oversaw construction on a hilltop site chosen for its natural defensive advantage while Sher Shah himself directed the effort from above. Sources differ slightly on the timeline, with construction generally dated to between 1541 and the late 1540s; Sher Shah died in 1545, before the fort was finished, and his successors completed the work.

The fort's name traces back to an earlier fortress, Rohtasgarh in present-day Bihar, India, which Sher Shah had captured from a local ruler in 1539 and after which he named his new Punjab stronghold.

## Strategic Purpose

Beyond blocking Humayun's return, the fort served a second, equally important function: suppressing the Gakhar tribes of the Potohar region, who had remained loyal to the Mughals and refused to submit to Sur authority. Positioned on the old route between Kabul and the Punjab plains — later absorbed into the Grand Trunk Road — the fort effectively controlled a key artery of trade and military movement between Central Asia and the subcontinent.

## Architecture and Layout

Rohtas Fort is enormous by any standard. Its walls run about 4 kilometres in circuit, enclosing roughly 12.63 acres, with 12 gates — four of them deliberately built as trap gates — 68 bastions, and close to 1,900 battlements. The layout is irregular rather than symmetrical, since the builders followed the natural contours of the hilltop rather than imposing a uniform geometric plan.

Several gates stand out individually. The Khwas Khani Gate, named after one of Sher Shah's generals, was the main entrance from the old Grand Trunk Road and features five battlements equipped with loopholes and machicolations. The Sohail Gate and Kabuli Gate are similarly monumental, while smaller gates like Gatalu and Tulla Mori served more localized or pedestrian traffic.

Inside, an interior wall separates the citadel from the rest of the complex, and the fort was self-sufficient in water through a system of *baolis*, or stepped wells built to keep the garrison supplied even under siege. Notable structures include the Shahi Masjid near the Kabuli Gate and the Haveli Man Singh, a residence added later during the Mughal period after the fort passed into Mughal hands. Architecturally, the fort is often described as an early and influential fusion of Central Asian military design with local South Asian building traditions — a blend that would go on to shape later Mughal architecture.

## UNESCO Recognition

Rohtas Fort was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1997, recognized as an exceptional example of early Muslim military architecture in Central and South Asia. UNESCO's citation highlights the fort's massive intact walls and gateways as evidence of its historical and architectural significance, and notes that despite centuries of political upheaval in the region, the fort itself was never taken by storm and has survived largely intact to the present day.

## Conservation Challenges

Survival from military assault has not meant survival from neglect. A persistent challenge is that a village has grown up inside the fort's walls over generations, which complicates both safety and preservation efforts even though a buffer zone of 750 to 1,500 metres now exists around the perimeter to protect the site's setting. Local accounts describe stone being taken from the fort's structures for use in nearby home construction, alongside encroachment by developers.

In response, the Rohtas Fort Conservation Programme was launched in 2000 by Pakistan's Archaeology and Museums department together with the Himalayan Wildlife Foundation, aiming to bring conservation and tourism management up to international standards, with particular attention to drainage and encroachment. Community-based initiatives have also emerged, including UNESCO- and STFP-backed programs training local youth in heritage safeguarding and sustainable tourism.

Most recently, in July 2026, UNESCO formally objected to restoration work being carried out at the site by Punjab's Directorate General of Archaeology and Museums, after a site visit prompted by third-party reports. The concern centered on the use of methods — including cement in wall repairs — that UNESCO considers inconsistent with accepted conservation standards for World Heritage properties, and officials noted this was not the first time Punjab's restoration methods had drawn such criticism, with a similar objection previously raised over work at Taxila. Observers have noted this could complicate Pakistan's efforts to nominate additional sites to the World Heritage list.

## Conclusion

Rohtas Fort's story is really two stories layered on top of each other: a 16th-century military triumph that was never breached in battle, and a 21st-century conservation effort still working out how to protect that triumph from slower threats — encroachment, weathering, and, more recently, restoration methods at odds with international heritage standards. Its scale and intact condition make it one of the most important surviving examples of early Muslim military architecture anywhere, and also one of the more instructive case studies in the ongoing work of keeping a living heritage site both authentic and inhabited.
